Voltage, Current, and Resistance: the three ideas everything else builds on

ASHWIN JUL 26, 2026 6 MIN READ

I'm starting completely from zero on power systems and power electronics, so before I write about anything more interesting — converters, transformers, the grid — I need to nail down the three ideas that everything else is built on: voltage, current, and resistance. This post is me working through them for the first time, in plain language.

The water pipe analogy

The most common way to build intuition for electricity is to compare it to water flowing through a pipe. It's not a perfect analogy, but it's good enough to get the three ideas straight.

Voltage is like the pressure a pump creates.

Current is like how much water actually flows through the pipe.

Resistance is like a narrow section of pipe that pushes back against that flow.

More pump pressure pushes more water through. A narrower pipe makes it harder for water to get through at the same pressure. Electricity behaves the same way — just with charge instead of water, and wires instead of pipes.

Voltage: the push

Voltage is the electrical "pressure" that pushes charge through a circuit. It's measured in volts (V). A 9V battery pushes harder than a 1.5V AA battery — it has more electrical pressure available. Voltage always exists between two points: a battery has a "+" and "−" terminal, and the voltage is the difference in pressure between them.

Current: the flow

Current is the actual movement of charge through a wire — how much is flowing, not how hard it's being pushed. It's measured in amperes, or "amps (A)." You can have a huge voltage pushing almost no current (like static shock from a doorknob — thousands of volts, but so little current it barely tingles), or a small voltage pushing a lot of current (like a car battery, which can deliver hundreds of amps at just 12V).

Resistance: the pushback

Resistance is whatever opposes the flow of current — measured in ohms (Ω). Thin wires, long wires, and certain materials all resist current more than thick, short, highly conductive ones. A resistor is a component built specifically to add a known, controlled amount of resistance to a circuit.

Ohm's Law: how the three connect

These three aren't independent — they're locked together by one equation, Ohm's Law:

Ohm's Law
V = I × R

In words: voltage equals current times resistance. If you know any two of the three, you can find the third. Rearranged, it also reads:

Solving for current
I = V / R

This version is the intuitive one: for a fixed resistance, more voltage means more current flows. And for a fixed voltage, more resistance means less current flows. That's the entire relationship — everything else is just this equation applied to different situations.

Worked example

A 9V battery is connected across a 3Ω resistor. How much current flows?

I = V / R = 9V / 3Ω = 3A

Three amps of current flow through the circuit.

Worked example

A circuit has 2A of current flowing through a 5Ω resistor. What's the voltage across it?

V = I × R = 2A × 5Ω = 10V

Power: where this connects to the rest of the blog

Voltage and current together determine power — the actual rate of energy being delivered, measured in watts (W). This is the equation that ties this post to literally everything else I'll write about here, because power systems and power electronics are, at the end of the day, about moving power around efficiently.

Power
P = V × I

A 100W light bulb running on 120V draws about 0.83A of current (from I = P / V). A phone charger delivering 5V at 2A is supplying 10W. Every future post — why the grid runs at high voltage, how a charger converts power, why transformers exist — is really just this equation showing up in a different context.
